[2026]

Fighting Words: Utilizing Populist Language as a Predictor in US Election Models

My honors thesis focused on the intersection between language and election outcomes. Specificly, I tested whether populist language could be used as a predictor for Congressional candidates' performances in election. Using a mixed-effects regression, I charted the relationship between the usage of populist words and tonality on social media by candidates against their margins of victory. I found that the populist tonality was significantly positively predictive of performance, with candidates who used more of this language performing better on average.

[2025]

Thank You For Your Attention

This article, published in the Brown Political Review, dissects the language quirks of President Trump through both a linguistic and poliltical lense. Analyzing his use of the phrase "thank you for your attention to this matter," I find that the inconsistent formal tone acts as a command to the listener, not simply asking for attention, but demanding respect.

[2024]

Investigating the Language and Sentiments in Discussions of Homelessness Across News Publications

This informal research, done in the Introduction to Corpus Linguistics course, assessed whether the linguistic portrayals of homelessness differed across news publications. Specifically, we investigated whether the political leaning and factual accuracy of the publication correlated to their mentions of crime and criminal behavior in articles on the homeless population. We found that political lean heavily predicted mentions of crime in these articles.
